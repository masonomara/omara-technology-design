# Homepage Research — O'Mara Technology

*Research for rewriting the homepage cover, structure, and overall narrative.*
*Reference agencies: Scope Labs (scopelabs.com) and Self Aware Studio (selfaware.studio)*
*Goal: meet in the middle — professional enough to be hired, human enough to be worth reading.*

---

## Scope Labs — Homepage Analysis

### What they say

**Headline:** "Building software for the next era"
**Sub:** "We're a team of founders and entrepreneurs shaping ambitious startups and forward-looking enterprises"

**Page structure:**
1. Hero — headline + sub + primary CTA
2. Service split — Startups / Enterprise / Applied AI / Incubator (with one-line descriptions each)
3. Stats strip — $30M+ raised, $350M+ client valuation, 1M+ users, 14 weeks average to launch
4. Case studies — 4 featured projects with hard metrics: $1.5M raised, $10M+ ARR, 250+ schools
5. Testimonials — clients describing Scope as "real partners," not contractors

**What they do well:**
- The stats strip works. "$30M+ raised by clients" is third-party proof presented in four words. It earns credibility without spending three paragraphs on it.
- The four service categories do real qualification work. You arrive knowing whether you're a startup, enterprise, or AI initiative — and you know Scope handles all three. The one-liners under each are short enough to be read, specific enough to be useful.
- Case study metrics are punchy. "50k+ students" tells you more than "a large user base." Scope doesn't over-describe what they built — they just tell you what happened.
- The incubator section is a smart flex. Building their own companies with the same tools they sell to clients is credibility without claiming it directly.

**What they do badly:**
- "Building software for the next era" is a headline that could belong to any software agency in the world. It claims magnitude without saying anything specific. You could shuffle it into a dozen competitor sites and no one would notice.
- "We're a team of founders and entrepreneurs" is the 2020s version of "passionate team." It's meant to signal legitimacy — "we've been in the arena" — but it's heard so often it's become noise.
- Superlatives throughout: "transformative," "pioneering," "revolutionizing," "forward-looking." These words have been drained of meaning by every pitch deck since 2015. By the time you read three, you stop reading.
- Nothing human. By the end of the homepage you know what Scope builds and who they've worked with. You have no sense of who they are. The writing is performing credibility rather than demonstrating it.
- The testimonials extend the page significantly without earning it. They're good testimonials but positioned as social proof insurance rather than natural punctuation.

**Tone verdict:** Corporate-confident. Speaks the language of investors and startup operators. Useful to study for structure and proof-point density. Not useful to copy at the word level.

## Self Aware Studio — Homepage Analysis

### What they say

**Headline:** "Self Aware is a design and technology studio that brings brands to life on the web."
**Positioning sub:** Based in Philadelphia. Creates "beautiful, performant websites for a wide range of individuals and organizations near and far."

**Page structure:**
1. Short self-description — one sentence, no drama
2. Five featured projects — name, one-line description, link
3. Recent achievements / activity (Launched PMA, podcast appearance, published document)
4. "View all projects" CTA
5. Contact prompt — email + form

**What they do well:**
- "Brings brands to life on the web" is specific. They're not building software for the next era — they're building websites. Saying what you actually do, with conviction, is rarer than it should be.
- The featured project descriptions are excellent: "redesigned website for a Chicago design institution founded in 1937" — that number (1937) is doing real work. It signals legacy, gravitas, complexity. It's one word and it tells you the whole story.
- "Beautiful, performant websites for a wide range of individuals and organizations near and far" sounds almost folksy — and that's the point. It's not trying to scale to enterprise. It's someone who makes good things and doesn't need to shout about it.
- "howdy@selfaware.studio" in the footer. The email address is itself a tone statement. It says: there are people here who thought about this.
- Recent activity section. Showing that they launched the Philadelphia Museum of Art site, appeared on a podcast, published a document — this is a studio that's alive and working. Not a portfolio page that was last updated in 2022.
- The restraint. They don't over-explain what they do or why you should hire them. They trust the reader to make the connection.

**What they do badly:**
- The homepage is almost too minimal. If the featured projects don't immediately communicate quality, there's nothing else doing the work. For an unknown visitor, the entry bar is high.
- "Beautiful, performant websites" is accurate but doesn't fully communicate ambition. Someone with a harder technical challenge — complex app, AI integration, systems thinking — might not see themselves in this description.
- No indication of what kind of client they want, what kind of budget is appropriate, what scale they work at. Self Aware trusts word-of-mouth to handle that filtering. That works when you have enough reputation. It leaves the homepage passive otherwise.
- The about page (from extended reading) is "We make things worth making" — which is genuinely great — but is so brief you're left without much to hold onto if you want to understand who this is and what they believe.

**Tone verdict:** Curatorial restraint. Work-first, identity second. Reads like someone who's confident enough not to try. The right voice for a studio with a strong aesthetic reputation. Less effective at communicating technical range or earning trust from clients who need to understand process before they commit.

## The Unique Problem of O'Mara's Homepage

O'Mara's homepage has something neither reference agency has: **an actual interactive game as the primary element.** This is a real differentiator — it says personality, craft, and playfulness in a way that no copy can claim. The risk is that it buries the agency pitch. The question isn't whether to keep the game. It's whether the cover does enough work before the game starts.

Currently, the cover shows:
- The wordmark
- "Strategy, design, and development for apps, websites, and software."
- Two buttons: "Start Game" and "Contact US"

That description is fine but generic. And "Contact US" is a weak second button — it asks for action before it gives a reason.

The cover has one job: tell someone who just arrived who you are, communicate enough personality that they're willing to engage, and give them a clear path into the work if the game isn't for them.

## The "Bean Shooter" Cover — What It Should Do

Your instinct to name the game is right. "Bean Shooter by O'Mara Technology and Design" immediately does something the current wordmark-only treatment doesn't: it makes the whole thing intentional. It signals that this wasn't an accident, that the game has a name, that someone built this deliberately. That's charming.

The bracket — "a ___ agency" — is where you can do real work. Options worth considering:

**Option A:** "a design and development studio"
Accurate. Doesn't over- or under-claim. Gets the job done.

**Option B:** "a small studio"
Self Aware does this kind of restraint well. "Small" signals access — you're not getting handed off. It's honest and rare in agency language.

**Option C:** "a product studio"
Scopes the work more precisely. "Product" signals you think in systems, not just deliverables. Scope Labs-adjacent but more honest at solo-practice scale.

**Option D:** "a one-person studio"
Risky but powerful. It's the most honest and the most differentiating thing you can say. Nobody says it because everyone is afraid to. Self Aware says "near and far" as a warmth gesture — this is the O'Mara equivalent.

**The three-button structure ("Start Game", "About", "View Work") is right.** It solves the current cover's biggest problem: you could arrive, click "Contact US," and never understand what you're contacting someone about. "About" and "View Work" give the visitor two real exits. They can learn who you are or see what you've made — both of which earn the contact action.

"Start Game" first is correct. It's the differentiating thing, so lead with it.

## What Self Aware Does That O'Mara Should Steal

1. **Short, confident self-description.** One sentence. Specific noun ("studio") + specific verb ("brings brands to life") + specific medium ("on the web"). Not a list of services.

2. **Featured work on the homepage.** Self Aware shows 5 projects on their homepage. It's not the full portfolio — it's a curated selection. The work is already done; this is just choosing which 3–4 projects represent you best.

3. **Activity as proof of being alive.** Self Aware shows recent achievements (launched PMA, appeared on podcast). The game leaderboard does this for O'Mara already — there are real scores from real people, which means someone is visiting and playing. That's a signal worth surfacing.

4. **The email as a tone object.** "howdy@selfaware.studio" is a minor genius move. info@omaratechnology.com is fine. But even the way you present it — "or reach me directly at info@omaratechnology.com" vs. a cold fallback footer — changes the feeling.

5. **Trust the work to carry weight.** Self Aware doesn't explain why they're good. They show the work. The Moor case study already does this — there are real decisions described, real stakes, real outcomes.

---

## What Scope Labs Does That O'Mara Should Steal

1. **The stats strip concept — but honest.** Scope shows "$30M+ raised by clients." You can't match that scale, and you shouldn't try to fake it. But you have real numbers: 500+ users, 50+ paying customers, App Store ratings, projects shipped. A three-number strip ("X projects shipped, X years of practice, X cities") earns trust without overselling.

2. **The service categories as qualification.** Not because O'Mara needs Scope's service page structure — but because giving visitors a fast way to self-identify ("this is for me") reduces friction before they ever contact you.

3. **The case study metrics as shorthand.** Scope's format of 3–4 hard numbers per project is genuinely useful. The Moor project has the data for this. Others may not, but even qualitative outcomes ("shipped to App Store in 14 weeks") work in this format.

4. **Wanting to be hired.** Scope makes it clear on every page that they want your business. O'Mara's current homepage doesn't do this explicitly — the game is fun but the ask gets lost. The contact path should be clear without being pushy.

## Proposed Homepage Structure

The game stays. The cover is the entry point. Below the game, a scrollable homepage section does the work the cover can't.

### Section 1 — Cover (the game)
```
Bean Shooter
by O'Mara Technology and Design
a [small / product] studio

[ Start Game ]   [ About ]   [ View Work ]
```

The existing video background of Mason plays behind this until the game starts. That's already correct — it establishes there's a real person here without explaining it.

### Section 2 — Below the fold (homepage scroll, AFTER the game area)
This only exists if you scroll past the game. It should do three things quickly:

**Who:**
One or two sentences that describe what O'Mara actually is. Not a tagline — a description. Honest about being a solo practice. Direct about the kind of work.

> "O'Mara Technology is Mason O'Mara — a product designer and developer based in Asbury Park, NJ. Strategy, design, and development for founders and teams building real things."

**What:**
3–4 featured projects. Project name, one specific line, cover image. Not the full portfolio — the best three. Link to work.

**How:**
The two engagement types (Strategic / Project) — very short. These are the qualification mechanism. A visitor who reads these two paragraphs knows whether they're the right client before they reach out.

**Where to go:**
One clear CTA at the bottom. Not a form — just a line and two links.

> "If something here looks right — [let's talk] or [see all the work]."

## Copy Voice Notes for the Rewrite

The goal is the voice O'Mara already has on the about page and services page — not the generic description on the game cover.

**Keep:**
- First-person. "Mason O'Mara is" or "I build" — not "O'Mara Technology provides"
- Specificity. "Asbury Park, NJ" is better than "based in New Jersey." Specific is credible.
- The honest acknowledgment of being one person. This is a feature, not a liability.
- The directness already in About.tsx: "Built to ship, not just to demo." "Nothing gets handed off to strangers." These lines are genuinely good.

**Cut:**
- "Strategy, design, and development for apps, websites, and software." — too list-like, sounds like a services menu
- Any version of "transformative," "innovative," "cutting-edge"
- Third-person self-description on the cover ("O'Mara Technology is...")
- The "Contact US" button with the capital S — this is a small thing but it communicates carelessness at the most-seen spot on the site

**Aim for:**
Self Aware's restraint + Scope's clarity about wanting to be hired. Short sentences. Specific nouns. No throat-clearing.

## The Agency Name Question

The cover currently says "O'Mara Technology" via wordmark. The user is considering "a ___ agency" in the cover subtitle.

"Agency" has baggage — it suggests scale, layers of people, process overhead. "Studio" is more honest for a solo practice and carries better connotations in the creative/product world. Self Aware uses "studio." That's worth borrowing.

"O'Mara Technology and Design" as the byline is accurate but formal. Could soften to "by Mason O'Mara" — more direct, less corporate, makes the one-person nature a feature.

## What NOT to Do

- Don't build a long homepage. The game is the homepage. Everything else is an explanation. Keep it short.
- Don't add testimonials to the homepage. The game leaderboard already provides social proof in a more interesting form — real people, real scores, real engagement.
- Don't try to match Scope's scale claims. "$350M+ client valuation" is their number; it's irrelevant to how O'Mara is positioned. Honest small numbers beat dishonest big claims.
- Don't bury the game. It is the most differentiating thing on the page. Everything else should support it, not compete with it.
- Don't add a services section to the homepage. The navigation has About, Work, Process, Contact — those pages do the service description work. The homepage is personality, not catalog.
- Don't change the video background — Mason's presence behind the cover is good and unusual. It says "real person" without saying it.
