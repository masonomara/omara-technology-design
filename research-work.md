# Work & Portfolio Research — O'Mara Technology

*Research for rewriting work index, project case study pages, and about page.*
*Reference agencies: Scope Labs (scopelabs.com) and Self Aware Studio (selfaware.studio)*

---

## Scope Labs

### Homepage & Overall Positioning

**Tagline:** "We Build the Future of Digital Products"
**Sub:** "Scope is a venture studio and software development agency that partners with founders and enterprises to design and build transformative digital products."

**Voice:** Confident to the point of claiming territory that doesn't belong to them. "Transformative," "pioneering," "revolutionizing" appear across the site. The pitch-deck confidence works when you're trying to get in a room — it doesn't work once someone's actually in the room and reads it twice.

**What they do well:**
- They sound like they want to be hired. Clear, direct positioning: "we build X for Y."
- They demonstrate range by naming client types (founders, enterprises, startups, AI companies).
- Metrics strips on case studies build credibility without much copy: "500% increase in user signups," "4.8 app store rating," hard numbers do the work.
- Strong testimonials placed throughout. Third-party voices carry weight.
- Cover images on the work index make it feel like a real portfolio, not a list.
- Case studies have a clear beginning, middle, and end — they tell a complete story.

**What they do badly:**
- Every single case study opens with: "[Client] partnered with Scope to build [X]." It's identical across every project. You feel the template before you feel the story.
- Body copy is robotic: passive constructions, over-reliance on "leveraged," "implemented," "developed." Nobody talks like this.
- "The Idea / The Partnership / The Solution" is a fine structure but when it's rigidly identical across all case studies, individual projects lose their personality.
- Superlatives mean nothing: "innovative approach," "seamless experience," "cutting-edge technology." These are filler.
- They don't let themselves be human. You read three case studies and still don't know who these people are.
- Hero headlines lean on vague corporate aspiration. "Revolutionizing recruitment through AI-powered matching" — this could describe 200 companies.

### Work Index (scopelabs.com/work)

- Grid layout with project cards and cover images. Clean, professional.
- Each card: client name, one-line description, category tags.
- Descriptions are short but generic: "AI-powered recruitment platform for the modern workforce."
- Filter by category (Web, Mobile, AI, etc.) — functional, probably useful.
- The index page is honest but forgettable. Gets the job done.

### Case Study Structure (recruitu, upnext, aigentless)

**Template:**
1. Hero image + client name + one-line description
2. "The Idea" — what the client wanted to build (2–4 sentences)
3. "The Partnership" — how Scope got involved (1–2 sentences, very formulaic)
4. "The Solution" — what they built (4–8 sentences, mostly technical spec language)
5. Metrics strip: 3–5 hard numbers
6. Testimonial from client
7. Deliverables list
8. More screenshots/mockups

**What works:** The metrics and testimonials sections are genuinely persuasive. The screenshots grid at the bottom is well executed — shows real product breadth.

**What doesn't:** The writing in sections 2–4 is undifferentiated across projects. You could shuffle the paragraphs between case studies and they'd still make sense. The individual story gets lost.

## Self Aware Studio

### Homepage & Overall Positioning

**Tagline (rotating/changing):** "Design studio making things for culture, commerce, and cause." / "We make things worth making."

**Voice:** Curatorial restraint. They're not trying to explain themselves to you — they trust the work to do it. The writing is fast, specific, and idiosyncratic. There's a point of view in every line.

**What they do well:**
- They write like people. "We make things worth making." Three words that tell you everything about their values without explaining them.
- The specificity is high. They reference individual details of projects — "the museum's 250-year archive" — instead of generalizing into "rich content strategy."
- Reading their copy doesn't feel like work. It's light enough to carry a mood.
- They communicate what they care about without turning it into a manifesto.
- The inquiry form copy is exceptional: "My name is [YOUR FULL NAME] and I am [YOUR ROLE] at [YOUR ORGANIZATION]." It's templated but it feels like a handshake, not a form.
- They name real collaborators and celebrate the client's role in the work.

**What they do badly:**
- Too minimal in some case studies. The Philadelphia Museum of Art entry — arguably their most prestigious credit — has almost no copy. The work speaks for itself only if you already know the work.
- The index page relies entirely on the work looking good. There's no scannable description or category — if the images don't communicate, the page doesn't communicate.
- Some project intros are so short they leave the reader without enough context to understand what was actually built.
- The editorial restraint occasionally tips into vagueness: "a new identity rooted in community" could mean anything.

### Work Index (selfaware.studio/work)

- Masonry or alternating grid of project images.
- Filter by category — simple, functional.
- No descriptions on hover, just the project name and client.
- Elegant but potentially alienating if the imagery doesn't immediately signal what you do.
- At smaller sizes, likely works better as a grid than a list.

### Case Study Structure (institute-of-design, philadelphia-museum-of-art, groove)

**Approach (variable, not templated):**
- Each project page has its own rhythm. Some lead with a bold contextual statement, some with an image, some with a short problem framing.
- Institute of Design: brief historical context → what changed → what they did → the result. Fast. Maybe 150 words of actual prose.
- Philadelphia Museum of Art: almost no prose — let the photography do the work. Works because the images are extraordinary.
- Groove: slightly more narrative — project origin, the specific design challenge, why the decisions were made.

**What works:** The absence of a rigid template means each case study feels like it's describing that specific project, not "a project." The best entries (Institute of Design, Groove) are short enough to read in 90 seconds and specific enough to feel earned.

**What doesn't:** The most minimal entries rely on readers already knowing the client's reputation. If you don't know the Philadelphia Museum of Art's design legacy, the page doesn't teach you anything.

## What O'Mara Does That Neither Agency Does

Reading the existing case studies (Moor in particular) reveals something neither reference agency does: **Mason explains the actual thinking behind design decisions**. Not just what was built, but why the algorithm works this way, why the database was structured like this, why website-before-app was the right call. This is rare and genuinely valuable to the right client. It's the thing worth preserving.

The risk is that it becomes a technical essay instead of a case study. Moor at 270 lines is closer to a long-form article than a portfolio piece. The writing is excellent — but the format doesn't serve a first-time visitor well.

Chomp at 5 lines is too lean. The work disappears.

The target is somewhere between them: enough to communicate the thinking, short enough to be read.

## Writing Voice Analysis

### Scope Labs — phrases to avoid
- "Partnered with [client] to build"
- "Leveraged cutting-edge technology"
- "Seamless user experience"
- "Innovative approach"
- "Revolutionizing / transforming / pioneering"
- "Robust platform"
- Any passive construction when active is available

### Self Aware Studio — phrases worth studying
- Specific historical/contextual detail before the pitch
- Short declarative sentences that carry weight
- Naming the constraint, not just the solution
- Crediting the client's contribution
- "Worth making" — value judgment, not capability claim

### What O'Mara already does right (keep it)
- First-person singular, not royal "we" on a solo practice
- Naming specific technical decisions and why
- Referencing real-world stakes (the boat show deadline, the tragedy in the news)
- Being honest about tradeoffs (website before app)
- Letting the client's outcome speak: "500+ users, 50+ paying customers"

### What to adjust
- Cut throat-clearing. Don't introduce a project, just start it.
- Lead with the most interesting thing, not the most general.
- Each project intro should be something you couldn't say about any other project.
- Let the decision-making show. The "why" is more compelling than the "what."
- End with the outcome, not a generic CTA embedded in the copy.

## Case Study Structure Recommendation

Based on this research, the structure that fits O'Mara's content and clients best:

**1. One-sentence hook**
The most specific, interesting thing about this project. Not "I built a marketplace" — something only true of this project.

**2. Context (2–4 sentences)**
Who the client is, what they were trying to do, and what made this hard or interesting. This is where Self Aware earns trust — specific historical or industry context makes the reader feel like you actually understand the space.

**3. What was built (2–4 sentences or brief section headers)**
What you actually made. Technical enough to signal competence, not so technical it loses a non-technical reader. Name the tools only when the choice is interesting.

**4. Key decisions (1–3 short sections, each 2–4 sentences)**
The part Scope Labs skips and Self Aware undervalues. What was the fork in the road? What did you decide and why? This is what O'Mara does better than either reference.

**5. Outcome**
Real numbers or real results if available. If not, describe what changed.

**6. Images**
Grid or masonry at the end, like Scope Labs. Show the breadth of the work.

## About Page — Voice Notes

Both agencies write about themselves in ways worth studying:

**Scope Labs:** "We are a team of designers, developers, and strategists who build digital products that make a difference." — Generic, forgettable.

**Self Aware:** doesn't over-explain their values. They demonstrate them by choosing what to show and how to describe it. The selection is the statement.

**For O'Mara:**
The current about page is close to right. The ethos paragraph ("Everything is changing faster than anyone can comfortably track") is honest and grounded. The capabilities grid does useful work. The bio section is good.

The thing to avoid: trying to sound like Scope Labs' confidence when you're a solo practice. The right positioning is: *one person, real craft, real access to that person.* That's actually rarer and more valuable than a studio's scale, if it's communicated clearly.

The engagement types (Strategic vs. Project) are the clearest expression of how to work with you. Lead with those more prominently — they do more qualification work than the capabilities grid.
